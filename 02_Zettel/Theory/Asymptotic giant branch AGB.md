name: Asymptotic giant branch AGB
description: double-shell burning, thermal pulses, third dredge-up, dust-driven mass loss and the planetary-nebula+WD endpoint
---

once core helium is exhausted at the end of the horizontal branch phase, low- and intermediate-mass stars ($0.8 \lesssim M/M_\odot \lesssim 8$) begin a second giant ascent, the **asymptotic giant branch**. its name comes from the early observation that the AGB approaches the red giant branch *asymptotically* in the CMD from the blue side at low $L$ and runs nearly parallel at high $L$, eventually exceeding the RGB tip in luminosity.

interior structure. the AGB star has, from inside out: an inert C/O core (the ashes of core He burning, electron-degenerate, $M_c \sim 0.5$ to $0.9 \, M_\odot$); a thin **He-burning shell** sitting on top of the core; an intershell region of pure He; a thin **H-burning shell** at the base of the H-rich envelope; and a deep convective envelope extended to $R \sim 100$ to $500 \, R_\odot$ along the Hayashi limit. the configuration is thus *double-shell burning*, the defining feature of AGB.

two sub-phases:

(1) **early AGB (E-AGB).** only the He shell burns; the H shell is temporarily extinguished or very weak. the star transitions from HB to AGB with steady He-shell burning above a contracting C/O core, climbing in $L$ at near-constant $T_\mathrm{eff}$. for intermediate-mass stars a *second dredge-up* occurs here, where the deep convective envelope penetrates into the He-rich intershell and brings He and N to the surface.

(2) **thermally-pulsing AGB (TP-AGB).** the H shell re-ignites and becomes the dominant luminosity source between pulses. but the He shell is geometrically thin and intrinsically *thermally unstable*: ash from H burning falls onto the He shell, the He-shell mass and temperature build up, and once a critical mass is reached the He shell ignites in a runaway flash (a **thermal pulse**). during a pulse, the He-shell luminosity briefly reaches $L_\mathrm{He} \sim 10^7$ to $10^8 \, L_\odot$ for $\sim 100$ yr, the intershell becomes convective and mixes burned material upward, the H shell is temporarily extinguished, and the envelope expands. between pulses ($\sim 10^4$ to $10^5$ yr apart) the H shell is the steady source. each pulse cycle slightly grows the C/O core.

**third dredge-up.** during the recovery phase after each thermal pulse, the convective envelope can dip into the intershell region, dredging up products of partial He burning to the surface. the dominant signature is **carbon enrichment**: $^{12}$C from the He-burning ashes is mixed into the photosphere, gradually raising the surface C/O ratio. once C/O exceeds unity, the star becomes a **carbon star** (C-type spectrum with $C_2$, CN, CH bands instead of TiO and VO bands). third dredge-up is also responsible for **s-process** element production: slow neutron captures on iron-peak seeds during the interpulse, with the neutron source being $^{13}\mathrm{C}(\alpha, n)^{16}\mathrm{O}$ in low-mass AGB stars and $^{22}\mathrm{Ne}(\alpha, n)^{25}\mathrm{Mg}$ at higher mass. observable s-process tracers include Ba, Sr, Y, Zr, La, technetium (only Tc-99 fresh from in-situ production proves an *intrinsic* AGB versus a binary-mass-transfer Ba star).

**hot bottom burning** (HBB) operates in stars with $M \gtrsim 4 \, M_\odot$: the base of the convective envelope is hot enough ($T \gtrsim 10^8$ K) to itself burn H via the CNO cycle, depleting carbon and producing nitrogen and lithium. HBB explains why intermediate-mass AGBs are *N-rich and Li-rich* rather than carbon stars; it also blocks the formation of carbon stars above $\sim 4 \, M_\odot$.

**mass loss.** the AGB is the dominant mass-loss phase for low-intermediate-mass stars. wind mechanisms, in order of increasing efficiency: chromospheric reimers wind on the lower AGB ($\dot M \sim 10^{-7} \, M_\odot$/yr); pulsation-amplified shocks at the base of the envelope, lofting material to where dust can condense; **dust-driven wind** at the upper AGB, where carbon dust (in C-rich envelopes) or silicate dust (in O-rich envelopes) absorbs stellar radiation and is accelerated outward, dragging the gas. on the upper AGB $\dot M$ can reach $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-5} \, M_\odot$/yr in the **superwind** phase. typical AGB stars lose $\Delta M \sim$ a few tenths of $M_\odot$ to several $M_\odot$ over $\sim 10^6$ yr.

**termination.** mass loss strips the H-rich envelope until only $\sim 10^{-3} \, M_\odot$ remains. the star detaches from the AGB, contracts to higher $T_\mathrm{eff}$ at near-constant $L$ (the *post-AGB* phase, $\sim 10^4$ yr), ionises the previously ejected material to make a **planetary nebula**, and finally cools onto the white dwarf cooling sequence as a C/O white dwarf with mass $M_\mathrm{WD} \sim 0.5$ to $1.0 \, M_\odot$ following the **initial-final mass relation** (kalirai et al.).

CMD signatures. AGB stars sit slightly *blueward* of the RGB at low $L$ but reach $L$ above the RGB tip. they are sparsely populated in low-mass clusters because the TP-AGB lifetime is short ($\sim 10^6$ yr); old MW GCs typically show a handful of AGB stars. the **AGB clump** or "AGB bump" is the analogue of the RGB bump for AGB stars. dusty AGBs are bright in mid-IR and provide the dominant *near-IR luminosity* of intermediate-age (1-2 Gyr) populations, complicating SSP models.
