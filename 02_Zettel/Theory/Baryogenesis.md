why is the universe made of matter and not antimatter? observationally, baryon-to-photon ratio
$$\eta_B \equiv \frac{n_B - n_{\bar B}}{n_\gamma} \approx 6 \times 10^{-10}$$

i.e. one extra baryon per billion photons; antimatter is essentially absent (no detectable antimatter galaxies, antimatter cosmic rays are at the level expected from secondary production in cosmic ray collisions).

inflation dilutes any pre-inflation baryon asymmetry by $e^{3N} \sim e^{180}$. so the asymmetry must be *generated dynamically* after inflation — this is **baryogenesis**.

---

## the Sakharov conditions (1967)

Andrei Sakharov showed that any process that produces a baryon asymmetry in an originally symmetric universe must satisfy three conditions:

1. **baryon number violation**: there must be a process that changes the net baryon number. (otherwise $\Delta B$ stays zero.)
2. **C and CP violation**: the rate of $X \to B$ must differ from the rate of $\bar X \to \bar B$. (otherwise any baryon excess from one channel is canceled by an equal antibaryon excess from the conjugate channel.)
3. **departure from thermal equilibrium**: in equilibrium, even with B-violation and CP-violation, the inverse process restores symmetry.

*all three* must be satisfied. the standard model has B-violation through electroweak sphalerons and CP-violation through the CKM matrix, but the CP-violation is too weak. so the SM by itself cannot generate the observed $\eta_B$.

→ baryogenesis requires **physics beyond the standard model**.

---

## baryogenesis scenarios

several proposed mechanisms, none confirmed:

### GUT baryogenesis
in grand unified theories, heavy gauge or Higgs bosons of mass $M_X \sim 10^{15}$ GeV decay out of equilibrium at $T \sim M_X$, producing a net baryon asymmetry. plausible but inflation must end at sufficiently high temperature for the X bosons to be produced.

### electroweak baryogenesis
during the electroweak phase transition at $T \sim 100$ GeV, sphaleron processes plus a strong first-order transition could produce $\eta_B$. requires either a strongly first-order EW transition (the SM Higgs is too heavy for this) or extra CP-violating physics.

### leptogenesis
heavy right-handed Majorana neutrinos decay out of equilibrium, producing a lepton asymmetry, which is then converted to a baryon asymmetry by sphalerons. attractive because right-handed neutrinos are also part of the see-saw explanation for tiny SM neutrino masses.

### Affleck-Dine
in supersymmetric theories, scalar fields carrying baryon number can have flat directions in the potential. classical field dynamics can produce baryon asymmetry. natural in many SUSY models.

### asymmetric dark matter
the baryon and dark matter asymmetries are linked: produced together by a common mechanism.

## experimental constraints

we know:
- $\eta_B \approx 6 \times 10^{-10}$ from BBN and CMB independently (see [BBN_baryon_to_photon_ratio](../../02_Zettel/Theory/BBN_baryon_to_photon_ratio.md) and [BBN_concordance_with_CMB](../../02_Zettel/Theory/BBN_concordance_with_CMB.md))
- the SM cannot produce this on its own
- proton decay has not been observed: $\tau_p > 10^{34}$ yr — constrains GUT couplings
- electroweak phase transition is *crossover* in the SM — no first-order transition

so we know:
- the baryon asymmetry is real and consistent across two epochs (BBN at $T \sim 1$ MeV, CMB at $T \sim 0.3$ eV)
- the standard model is not enough
- something beyond the SM is responsible

but **we do not know** which mechanism. determining this is one of the major goals of particle physics + cosmology in the next decades.
