> The SKA is the ultimate next-generation radio observatory, combining unprecedented collecting area and computing power across two continents.

---

## core physical intuition

![Square Kilometre Array (SKA): next-generation radio observatory spanning Australia and South Africa.](The_Square_Kilometre_Array_SKA.png)


Radio signals from the early universe and distant galaxies are incredibly faint. To detect them, you need an enormous amount of metal pointing at the sky to collect the photons. The Square Kilometre Array aims to build a total collecting area approaching one square kilometer, vastly surpassing any existing instrument.

Because different science goals require observing at different frequencies, the SKA is split into two entirely different types of arrays. The low-frequency array looks like fields of metal wire antennas to catch long wavelengths, while the mid-frequency array uses traditional steerable dishes. By networking thousands of these receivers together over baselines stretching hundreds of kilometers, the SKA will provide extreme sensitivity and high angular resolution, generating exabytes of data that will push the limits of modern supercomputing.

---

## key specs

The observatory is being constructed in two phases. SKA-Mid is located in South Africa and covers 350 MHz to 15 GHz. It will consist of 197 new 15-meter dishes integrated with the existing 64 dishes of the MeerKAT array, with maximum baselines around 150 km. 

SKA-Low is located in Western Australia and covers 50 to 350 MHz. It will deploy a staggering 131,072 dipole antennas grouped into 512 stations, with baselines up to 65 km. Together, these arrays are expected to be roughly 50 times more sensitive than the current Very Large Array.

---

## astrophysical context

The sheer scale of the SKA enables revolutionary science cases. It is designed to map the distribution of neutral hydrogen during the Epoch of Reionization, effectively watching the first stars turn on. It will act as a galaxy-sized gravitational wave detector by monitoring networks of rapidly spinning pulsars. It will map cosmic magnetism across the universe and search for complex, prebiotic molecules in the cradles of newly forming star systems. First science operations are expected in the late 2020s.
