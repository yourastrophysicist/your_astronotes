> Multi-telescope optical arrays can synthesize actual pictures of stellar surfaces, revealing starspots, convection cells, and rapid rotation.

---

## core physical intuition

![Stellar surface imaging: reconstructing starspots and rapid rotation flattening from optical interferometry data.](Stellar_surface_imaging.jpg)


While measuring a single diameter is useful, combining light from many telescopes at once allows you to mathematically reconstruct a true two-dimensional picture of a star's surface. This requires extensive baseline coverage to sample many spatial frequencies and the use of closure phases to bypass the corrupting effects of Earth's atmosphere.

When an array has enough telescopes, the resulting images reveal that stars are not featureless glowing spheres. You can physically see the darker bands caused by rapid rotation throwing the star's equator outward and cooling it down, a phenomenon known as gravity darkening. You can also map giant magnetic starspots or the massive, bubbling convection cells that dominate the surfaces of red supergiants. 

---

## key results

Stellar surface imaging has produced several landmark results in modern astrophysics. Using the MIRC instrument at the CHARA array, astronomers imaged the rapidly rotating star Altair, confirming its highly oblate, flattened shape. At the VLTI, the PIONIER instrument mapped the surface of Betelgeuse and Antares, directly revealing huge, asymmetric convective hotspots bubbling up from their interiors. Similar techniques have resolved the inner rims of protoplanetary disks around young T Tauri stars.

---

## astrophysical context

Stellar surface imaging is one of the most unique and powerful capabilities of optical and near-infrared interferometry. No other observational technique, not even the largest planned single-mirror extremely large telescopes, can achieve the fractional milliarcsecond angular resolution required to actually resolve the distinct surface features of other stars.
