title: "A Brief History of X-ray and Gamma-ray Missions"
---

The universe hides most of its violence from our eyes
	optical light tells only a small fraction of the story
		the hottest, most energetic, and most violent phenomena
			emit almost exclusively in **X-rays** and **gamma rays**

But there is a problem:
	the Earth's atmosphere is **opaque** to these photons
		a photon with energy above $\sim 10~\text{eV}$ is absorbed before reaching the ground
			X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy could only begin once humanity reached space

This is the story of how we built the eyes to see the invisible universe

---

## Chapter 1 — The First Glimpse: Rockets (1949–1962)

The story begins not with satellites, but with **sounding rockets**
	brief suborbital flights carrying detectors above most of the atmosphere
		each giving only a few minutes of observation before falling back to Earth

In **1949**, Herbert Friedman detected X-ray emission from the Sun using a V-2 rocket
	proving the concept: the X-ray universe was real and within reach

The decisive moment came on **June 18, 1962**
	Riccardo Giacconi and colleagues launched an **Aerobee 150 rocket** from White Sands
		their target was the Moon
			but what they found changed astronomy forever:
				an intense, diffuse X-ray source in the direction of **Scorpius**
					named **Sco X-1**, the first extrasolar X-ray source ever detected
						and a diffuse X-ray background permeating the entire sky

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/AerobeeHi.jpg/250px-AerobeeHi.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">An Aerobee Hi sounding rocket at White Sands Missile Range Museum — the same type of vehicle that carried Giacconi's detectors to the edge of space in 1962, opening the era of X-ray astronomy. Giacconi was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2002 for this discovery.</font>

That 1962 flight lasted only **350 seconds** above the atmosphere
	yet it was enough to reveal that the universe was far more active in X-rays than anyone had imagined
		the sky was not dark, it was blazing

---

## Chapter 2 — The Accidental Discovery: Gamma-Ray Bursts (1967)

While the X-ray astronomers were building their next rockets,
	the US military launched the **Vela satellites**
		designed to monitor for nuclear weapon tests from space

On **July 2, 1967**, the Vela 4 satellite detected something unexpected:
	a brief, intense flash of gamma rays
		coming not from Earth, but from deep space
			this was the **first gamma-ray burst (GRB)** ever detected

The discovery was declassified and published only in **1973**
	the origin of GRBs would remain one of astronomy's greatest mysteries for another 30 years

---

## Chapter 3 — The First Sky Survey: Uhuru (1970)

The rocket era made clear that a **dedicated satellite** was needed
	something that could stare at the sky continuously and map the full X-ray sky

On **December 12, 1970**, NASA launched the **Small Astronomy Satellite A**
	from the San Marco platform off the coast of **Kenya**
		in recognition of Kenya's hospitality, the satellite was named **Uhuru**
			the Swahili word for freedom

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8a/X-Ray_Explorer_Satellite.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Uhuru satellite (SAS-1 / Explorer 42), the first spacecraft dedicated entirely to X-ray astronomy. Its two proportional counter detectors scanned the sky continuously for three years, producing the first comprehensive catalog of X-ray sources.</font>

Uhuru carried two **proportional counters** sensitive to $2$–$20~\text{keV}$ photons
	and for three years it systematically scanned the entire sky
		producing the **4U catalog** containing $\sim 339$ X-ray sources

Among its discoveries:
	the first evidence for **X-ray binary systems**
		where a neutron star or black hole accretes matter from a companion star
	the first detection of **X-ray pulsars** (Her X-1)
		with a spin period of $1.24~\text{s}$, confirming neutron stars existed
	evidence for **extended X-ray emission** from galaxy clusters
		revealing the hot intracluster medium ($T \sim 10^8~\text{K}$)

Uhuru could not form images
	it only measured the total X-ray flux from each direction
		but it proved the sky was rich with X-ray sources
			and created the roadmap for all future missions

---

## Chapter 4 — The Eye Opens: Einstein Observatory (1978)

Counting photons was not enough
	astronomers wanted to **see** the X-ray universe in detail

The breakthrough came with the **Einstein Observatory** (HEAO-2)
	launched on **November 13, 1978**
		it was the first satellite to carry a fully **imaging X-ray telescope**
			using nested **Wolter Type I** grazing incidence mirrors

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/HEAO-2_High_Energy_Astronomy_Observatory_0102090.jpg/500px-HEAO-2_High_Energy_Astronomy_Observatory_0102090.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Einstein Observatory (HEAO-2) at NASA. It was the first space telescope to use focusing grazing incidence optics to form true X-ray images, operating in the $0.2$–$4~\text{keV}$ band from 1978 to 1981.</font>

Einstein could resolve X-ray sources with angular resolution of $\sim 4''$
	orders of magnitude better than any previous X-ray instrument

What it saw transformed our understanding:
	**Cygnus X-1** revealed as a compact X-ray binary containing a black hole candidate

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/HEAO-2_Image_of_the_Black_Hole%2C_Cygnus_X-1%2C_Taken_by_the_High_Energy_Astronomy_Observatory_8003548.jpg/500px-HEAO-2_Image_of_the_Black_Hole%2C_Cygnus_X-1%2C_Taken_by_the_High_Energy_Astronomy_Observatory_8003548.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">Einstein Observatory image of Cygnus X-1, one of the strongest X-ray sources in the sky and the first strong black hole candidate. The surrounding diffuse emission traces hot gas in the interstellar medium.</font>

	X-ray jets from **active galactic nuclei** were imaged for the first time
	thousands of faint point sources were detected
		revealing that virtually every type of astronomical object emits X-rays
	the **extragalactic X-ray background** was partially resolved into discrete sources

Einstein operated until **1981**, demonstrating the power of X-ray imaging
	and setting the template for all imaging X-ray telescopes that followed

---

## Chapter 5 — Mapping the Hot Universe: ROSAT (1990)

With Einstein proving the value of X-ray imaging,
	the next step was to combine imaging with an **all-sky survey**
		something Uhuru had done in the 1970s, but now with focusing optics

The **ROSAT** (Röntgensatellit) mission, a German/US/UK collaboration,
	was launched on **June 1, 1990**

Its first task was the **ROSAT All-Sky Survey (RASS)**:
	for six months it scanned the entire sky with focusing optics
		in the soft X-ray band $0.1$–$2.4~\text{keV}$

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Delta_6920-10_launch_with_ROSAT.jpg/400px-Delta_6920-10_launch_with_ROSAT.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">Launch of the ROSAT satellite on a Delta rocket from Cape Canaveral, June 1990. ROSAT would go on to detect over 125,000 X-ray sources in its all-sky survey — nearly 400 times more than Uhuru's 4U catalog.</font>

The RASS detected more than **125,000 X-ray sources**
	nearly 400 times more than the entire Uhuru catalog
		in just six months of scanning

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/ROSAT_Skymap_of_X-ray_Sources.jpg/600px-ROSAT_Skymap_of_X-ray_Sources.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The ROSAT all-sky survey in X-rays. The plane of the Milky Way runs horizontally through the center. The diffuse soft X-ray background, hot plasma in our Galaxy, and thousands of point sources (AGN, stars, X-ray binaries) are all visible in this map.</font>

After the survey phase, ROSAT spent eight years in pointed observations
	its soft X-ray sensitivity made it ideal for studying:
		**clusters of galaxies** and their hot intracluster gas
		**isolated neutron stars** (the Magnificent Seven)
		**stellar coronae** and X-ray emission from normal stars

ROSAT operated until **1999**, leaving a legacy that defined the soft X-ray sky

---

## Chapter 6 — Gamma Rays from the Most Violent Events: CGRO (1991)

While ROSAT was mapping soft X-rays,
	NASA launched the **Compton Gamma Ray Observatory (CGRO)**
		deployed from Space Shuttle Atlantis on **April 5, 1991**
			the second of NASA's four Great Observatories

CGRO carried four instruments covering an enormous energy range
	from $20~\text{keV}$ all the way to $30~\text{GeV}$:
		**BATSE**: eight scintillation detectors monitoring the full sky for bursts
		**OSSE**: oriented scintillation spectrometer
		**COMPTEL**: Compton telescope for $1$–$30~\text{MeV}$ (see [Compton Telescope](../../02_Zettel/Theory/Compton Telescope.html))
		**EGRET**: high-energy gamma-ray telescope, $20~\text{MeV}$–$30~\text{GeV}$

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d9/CGRO_s37-96-010.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Compton Gamma Ray Observatory deployed in orbit in 1991. At 17 tonnes it was the heaviest astrophysical payload ever launched at the time. It operated for nine years until a controlled deorbit in June 2000.</font>

CGRO's greatest contribution was solving the mystery of **gamma-ray bursts**
	BATSE detected **2704 GRBs** over nine years
		their distribution across the sky was perfectly **isotropic**
			ruling out a Galactic origin once and for all
				GRBs had to come from cosmological distances

EGRET produced the first all-sky map at energies above $100~\text{MeV}$:

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Moon_egret.jpg/300px-Moon_egret.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Moon as seen by EGRET at energies above 20 MeV, produced by cosmic rays hitting the lunar surface. This image illustrates EGRET's ability to detect gamma-ray emission from individual sources — the same instrument mapped the entire gamma-ray sky and catalogued hundreds of blazars and pulsars.</font>

CGRO's legacy was the proof that the high-energy universe was a place of continuous, extreme violence
	powered by the most energetic phenomena known: GRBs, blazars, and pulsars

---

## Chapter 7 — The Mystery Solved: BeppoSAX and GRB Afterglows (1996)

CGRO showed that GRBs were cosmological, but their nature remained unknown
	the bursts were too brief and too poorly localized for follow-up observations

The Italian/Dutch satellite **BeppoSAX**, launched on **April 30, 1996**,
	carried a combination of instruments covering $0.1$–$300~\text{keV}$:
		a Wide Field Camera (WFC) for rapid localization
		X-ray concentrators and proportional counters for spectroscopy

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8f/BeppoSAX.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">Artist's conception of BeppoSAX in orbit. The satellite's combination of wide-field and narrow-field instruments allowed it to detect a GRB with the WFC and immediately slew the focusing telescopes to the same position — a capability that proved decisive in cracking the GRB mystery.</font>

On **February 28, 1997**, BeppoSAX detected **GRB 970228**
	and within eight hours, its narrow-field X-ray telescope was pointing at the same position
		revealing a fading **X-ray afterglow** for the first time
			allowing optical telescopes to follow up and measure a redshift: $z = 0.695$

GRBs were confirmed to be at **cosmological distances**
	their isotropic energy release was $\sim 10^{51}$–$10^{53}~\text{erg}$
		making them the most luminous events in the universe

BeppoSAX solved in a single observation what had been a mystery for 25 years
	the source of gamma-ray bursts: the death of massive stars and mergers of compact objects

---

## Chapter 8 — The Golden Age: Chandra and XMM-Newton (1999)

The year **1999** was the turning point of X-ray astronomy
	two major observatories were launched within five months of each other
		together they transformed the field beyond recognition

### Chandra X-ray Observatory

Launched on **July 23, 1999**, from Space Shuttle Columbia,
	NASA's **Chandra** (named after Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar)
		achieved what no X-ray telescope had before:
			**sub-arcsecond angular resolution**

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fd/Chandra_artist_illustration.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">Artist's illustration of the Chandra X-ray Observatory. Its four pairs of nested Wolter Type I mirrors are ground and polished to nanometer precision, giving it an angular resolution of $\sim 0.5''$ — comparable to the Hubble Space Telescope in optical light. It operates in the $0.1$–$10~\text{keV}$ band.</font>

Chandra's mirrors were polished to nanometer precision
	achieving a half-power diameter of $\sim 0.5''$
		a factor of $\sim 10$ better than any previous X-ray telescope

Its most iconic result: the **Bullet Cluster** (2006)
	the first direct observational evidence for **dark matter**

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/Bullet_cluster.jpg/500px-Bullet_cluster.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Bullet Cluster (1E 0657-558): a composite of Chandra X-ray data (pink, hot gas) and optical imaging with gravitational lensing mass maps (blue, total mass). The separation of the X-ray-emitting gas from the dominant mass component during the cluster collision provides the most direct evidence for the existence of dark matter.</font>

### XMM-Newton

Launched on **December 10, 1999**, by ESA on an Ariane 5 rocket,
	**XMM-Newton** (X-ray Multi-Mirror Mission) took a complementary approach:
		instead of resolution, it maximized **collecting area**

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/XMM-Newton_spacecraft_model.png/500px-XMM-Newton_spacecraft_model.png)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The XMM-Newton spacecraft model. Three co-aligned Wolter Type I mirror assemblies — each with 58 nested shells — give XMM-Newton an effective area of $\sim 4500~\text{cm}^2$ at $1~\text{keV}$, the largest of any X-ray observatory. The optical monitor (OM) enables simultaneous UV/optical observations.</font>

XMM-Newton's three mirror assemblies, each with 58 nested Wolter shells,
	collect more X-ray photons than any telescope before or since
		enabling:
			high-resolution **grating spectroscopy** via the Reflection Grating Spectrometer (RGS) (see [X-ray diffraction grating](../../02_Zettel/Theory/X-ray diffraction grating.html))
			simultaneous **optical/UV monitoring** with the Optical Monitor
			deep surveys revealing the population of AGN across cosmic time

Together, Chandra and XMM-Newton define the state of the art in soft X-ray astronomy
	complementary in design, each revealing aspects of the universe invisible to the other

---

## Chapter 9 — Hunting Gamma-Ray Bursts in Real Time: Swift (2004)

The GRB afterglow era opened by BeppoSAX demanded a faster, more autonomous spacecraft
	one that could detect a burst, localize it, and repoint within seconds

NASA's **Swift** mission, launched on **November 20, 2004**,
	was designed as the ultimate GRB hunter:
		**BAT** (Burst Alert Telescope): coded mask covering $15$–$200~\text{keV}$, field of view $\sim 1.4~\text{sr}$ (see [Coded Mask](../../02_Zettel/Theory/Coded Mask.html))
		**XRT** (X-Ray Telescope): focusing Wolter optics, $0.3$–$10~\text{keV}$
		**UVOT** (UV/Optical Telescope): simultaneous multiwavelength coverage

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Swift_Observatory_spacecraft_model.png/500px-Swift_Observatory_spacecraft_model.png)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Swift spacecraft. When BAT detects a GRB, Swift autonomously slews within 60 seconds to point the XRT and UVOT at the burst, providing X-ray and UV/optical positions to ground observers worldwide within minutes. This automated response system is what makes Swift uniquely powerful for time-domain astronomy.</font>

Swift's autonomy was its revolution:
	upon detecting a burst with BAT, the spacecraft **automatically slews** within $\sim 60~\text{s}$
		allowing XRT and UVOT to capture the afterglow while still bright

By 2015, Swift had detected more than **1000 GRBs**:

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fc/GRBs_detected_by_Swift%2C_2004-2015.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">All-sky distribution of gamma-ray bursts detected by Swift's BAT between 2004 and 2015. The isotropic distribution confirms the cosmological origin of GRBs established by CGRO/BATSE. Each point represents a burst for which Swift obtained an X-ray and often an optical afterglow position — enabling redshift measurements and host galaxy identifications.</font>

Swift proved that GRBs come in two populations:
	**long GRBs** ($T_{90} > 2~\text{s}$): collapse of massive stars (collapsars)
	**short GRBs** ($T_{90} < 2~\text{s}$): merger of two neutron stars or a neutron star and a black hole

## Chapter 10 — The Soft Gamma-Ray Sky: INTEGRAL (2002)

Filling the gap between hard X-rays and MeV gamma-rays,
	ESA launched **INTEGRAL** (INTErnational Gamma-Ray Astrophysics Laboratory)
		on **October 17, 2002**, from Baikonur Cosmodrome on a Proton rocket

INTEGRAL covers $15~\text{keV}$–$10~\text{MeV}$
	with its main instrument IBIS/ISGRI using a **coded mask** over a CdTe detector
		(see [Coded Mask](../../02_Zettel/Theory/Coded Mask.html))

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/INTEGRAL_spacecraft_model.png/500px-INTEGRAL_spacecraft_model.png)
<font color="#bfbfbf">Artist's impression of the INTEGRAL spacecraft. INTEGRAL's highly eccentric orbit (perigee $\sim 9000~\text{km}$, apogee $\sim 153,000~\text{km}$, period $\sim 3~\text{days}$) keeps it above the radiation belts for most of its orbit, enabling long, uninterrupted observations.</font>

INTEGRAL's major contributions include:
	the first complete hard X-ray all-sky survey above $17~\text{keV}$
		revealing a population of **heavily obscured AGN** invisible to soft X-ray telescopes
	detailed mapping of **$^{26}\text{Al}$ and $^{60}\text{Fe}$** radioactive decay lines
		tracing nucleosynthesis and supernova activity in the Milky Way
	monitoring of **galactic black hole transients** and neutron star X-ray binaries
	electromagnetic counterpart observations during the **gravitational wave era** (GW170817)

---

## Chapter 11 — The High-Energy Sky Revealed: Fermi (2008)

Gamma-ray astronomy above $100~\text{MeV}$ had been limited by EGRET's modest sensitivity
	the field was waiting for a successor with orders-of-magnitude improvement

NASA's **Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope**, launched on **June 11, 2008**,
	delivered exactly that:

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/Fermi_Gamma-ray_Space_Telescope_spacecraft_model.png/500px-Fermi_Gamma-ray_Space_Telescope_spacecraft_model.png)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope spacecraft. The LAT instrument (Large Area Telescope) covers the top of the spacecraft; its pair-conversion technique and wide field of view allow it to monitor the entire sky above 20 MeV every three hours, providing an unprecedented continuous census of high-energy sources.</font>

Fermi carries two instruments:
	**LAT** (Large Area Telescope): $20~\text{MeV}$–$300~\text{GeV}$, pair-conversion telescope
		field of view $\sim 2.4~\text{sr}$, surveys the entire sky every $\sim 3~\text{hours}$
	**GBM** (Gamma-ray Burst Monitor): $8~\text{keV}$–$40~\text{MeV}$
		continuing the BATSE legacy of monitoring the full sky for bursts

After five years of operation, the LAT sky looked like this:

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Fermi_5_year_11000x6189.png/800px-Fermi_5_year_11000x6189.png)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The Fermi LAT five-year all-sky map above 1 GeV. The bright band is the Galactic plane, dominated by diffuse emission from cosmic-ray interactions with interstellar gas. Hundreds of individual sources are visible: blazars, pulsars, supernova remnants, and star-forming galaxies. This map contains roughly 10 times more sources than EGRET's full catalog.</font>

Fermi's major discoveries:
	the **Fermi Bubbles**: two giant gamma-ray lobes extending $50,000~\text{light-years}$ above and below the Galactic center
	hundreds of new **blazars** (AGN with jets pointed at us)
	more than 200 **gamma-ray pulsars**
	detection of gamma rays from the **Crab Nebula flares**
	the first simultaneous gravitational wave and gamma-ray detection: **GW170817 + GRB 170817A**
		the smoking gun for short GRBs as neutron star mergers

## Chapter 12 — Focusing the Hard Universe: NuSTAR (2012)

One frontier remained: the **hard X-ray sky** above $10~\text{keV}$
	previous missions in this band (INTEGRAL, Swift/BAT) used coded masks
		giving arcminute to degree-scale resolution at best

On **June 13, 2012**, NASA launched **NuSTAR** (Nuclear Spectroscopic Telescope Array)
	the first focusing telescope sensitive above $10~\text{keV}$, covering $3$–$79~\text{keV}$

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/NuSTAR_spacecraft_model.png/500px-NuSTAR_spacecraft_model.png)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The NuSTAR spacecraft in its deployed configuration. A $10~\text{m}$ deployable mast separates the optics (multilayer-coated Wolter mirrors, see [Bragg Reflection](../../02_Zettel/Theory/Bragg Reflection.html)) from the focal plane detectors. This long focal length is necessary to focus hard X-rays at grazing incidence angles small enough to reflect photons above 10 keV.</font>

NuSTAR uses **multilayer mirror coatings** (alternating layers of Pt/C and W/Si)
	to extend grazing incidence reflection up to $79~\text{keV}$
		achieving $\sim 18''$ angular resolution in the hard X-ray band

Its key results:
	mapping **radioactive $^{44}\text{Ti}$** in Cassiopeia A, constraining the supernova explosion mechanism
	measuring the **spin of supermassive black holes** through iron K$\alpha$ line profile fitting
	deep hard X-ray surveys resolving the bulk of the cosmic X-ray background above $10~\text{keV}$
	detecting **pulsations from ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULX)**
		revealing neutron stars accreting at super-Eddington rates

## Chapter 13 — The Deepest Survey: eROSITA (2019)

The ROSAT all-sky survey had defined the soft X-ray sky for 30 years
	in 2019, it was finally surpassed

The **eROSITA** instrument on board the Russian-German **SRG spacecraft**,
	launched on **July 13, 2019**,
		completed the deepest all-sky X-ray survey in history

In $0.2$–$8~\text{keV}$, eROSITA:
	is **more sensitive** than ROSAT by a factor of $\sim 20$ at soft X-rays
	has detected more than **900,000 sources** in its first all-sky survey
		compared to ROSAT's 125,000

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/43/SRG-eROSITA_all-sky_image.jpg)
<font color="#bfbfbf">The eROSITA all-sky survey — the deepest X-ray all-sky image ever made. Colors encode photon energy: red for 0.3–0.6 keV, green for 0.6–1 keV, blue for 1–2.3 keV. The image reveals the hot baryonic universe: thousands of galaxy clusters tracing large-scale structure, millions of AGN, stellar coronae, and diffuse hot gas in the Milky Way and Magellanic Clouds.</font>

eROSITA's primary science goal is a **census of galaxy clusters** across cosmic time
	providing cosmological constraints on dark energy and the growth of cosmic structure
		through the abundance and evolution of the most massive gravitationally bound objects

---

## Epilogue — The Universe in All Its Violence

From Giacconi's 350-second rocket flight in 1962
	to eROSITA's year-long all-sky survey cataloguing millions of sources,
		X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy have revealed a universe of extremes:

**X-ray missions:**
	Uhuru (1970) $\to$ Einstein (1978) $\to$ ROSAT (1990) $\to$ ASCA (1993) $\to$ BeppoSAX (1996) $\to$ Chandra/XMM-Newton (1999) $\to$ NuSTAR (2012) $\to$ eROSITA (2019)

**Gamma-ray missions:**
	Vela (1967) $\to$ CGRO (1991) $\to$ INTEGRAL (2002) $\to$ Swift (2004) $\to$ Fermi (2008)

The next chapter is already being written:
	**Athena** (ESA, planned $\sim 2035$): $40\times$ more effective area than XMM-Newton
		with a microcalorimeter spectrometer achieving $\Delta E \sim 2.5~\text{eV}$ resolution
	**AXIS** (NASA concept): sub-arcsecond wide-field survey successor to Chandra

Each generation of instruments has revealed phenomena invisible to the previous one
	the universe in high-energy light is still full of surprises waiting to be seen
