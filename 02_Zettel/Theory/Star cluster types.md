name: Star cluster types
description: open vs globular clusters, their host-galaxy categories, and the giant molecular cloud origin shared by both
---

a star cluster is a gravitationally bound, coeval, chemically (mostly) homogeneous group of stars formed from the collapse and fragmentation of a single giant molecular cloud. operationally, clusters are sorted into two morphological families that differ in essentially every property except their formation channel.

**open clusters** (galactic clusters in the older literature) are loose, irregular, low-density systems with $N \sim 10^2$ to $10^3$ stars, half-mass radii of $1$ to $5$ pc, and total masses of $10^2$ to $10^4 \, M_\odot$. they sit in the disk of their host galaxy, share the disk's metallicity (roughly solar in the milky way), and are *young*: ages span $\lesssim 1$ Myr (embedded clusters like the orion nebula cluster) up to a few Gyr at most for the long-lived survivors like NGC 188. they are weakly bound, with crossing times comparable to their dissolution timescale, and most evaporate into the field within $\sim 10^8$ yr through tidal stripping, internal two-body relaxation, and disk-shocking. canonical examples are the pleiades ($\sim 100$ Myr), hyades ($\sim 600$ Myr) and NGC 3532.

**globular clusters** are dense, spheroidal, populous systems with $N \sim 10^4$ to $10^6$ stars, half-mass radii of $\sim 3$ pc, central densities up to $10^5 \, M_\odot \, \mathrm{pc}^{-3}$, and total masses $10^4$ to $10^6 \, M_\odot$. they are old ($\gtrsim 10$ Gyr), metal-poor ($-2.5 \lesssim [\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}] \lesssim -0.5$), tightly bound, and live in the halo and bulge of their host galaxy on long, eccentric orbits. see [Globular clusters as SSP laboratories](../../02_Zettel/Theory/Globular clusters as SSP laboratories.md) for the GC-as-laboratory framing.

beyond this binary split there is a useful taxonomy by host environment: clusters belonging to the milky way, to local-group dwarfs (LMC, SMC, fornax, sagittarius), to nearby spirals like M31 (PHAT-mapped) and M33, and to ellipticals like M87 (which hosts $\sim 10^4$ GCs). the *number of clusters per galaxy*, $N_\mathrm{gal}$, scales roughly with halo mass; ellipticals are GC-rich, dwarfs are GC-poor, and the specific frequency $S_N$ (clusters per unit galaxy luminosity) is itself a probe of assembly history.

both families share the same formation channel: turbulent fragmentation inside a giant molecular cloud, supersonic compression, and rapid (a few Myr) star formation before the residual gas is expelled by the first OB winds and supernovae. what differs is the *initial mass and density* of the natal cloud. a $\sim 10^5 \, M_\odot$ progenitor cloud at $\sim 10^3 \, \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ produces an open cluster; a $\sim 10^7 \, M_\odot$ progenitor at $> 10^4 \, \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ (conditions plausibly common at $z > 2$ but rare today) produces a globular. this picture connects to the bound-cluster fraction $\Gamma$ that varies with the SFR surface density of the host (kruijssen 2012).

a third loose category, **stellar associations** (OB associations, T associations), are unbound siblings that disperse on a single crossing time; they are not strictly clusters but share the same molecular-cloud origin and are coeval, so their CMDs are still readable as approximate SSPs.

a final observational pitfall: very young, massive, embedded clusters in the LMC (R136, NGC 2070) and in M82 are sometimes called "young massive clusters" or "super star clusters". their masses overlap the GC range, but their ages are $\lesssim 100$ Myr; whether they will survive to become old GCs depends on the residual-gas-expulsion phase and the tidal field of the host. they are the closest present-day analogues of what proto-GC formation might have looked like.
