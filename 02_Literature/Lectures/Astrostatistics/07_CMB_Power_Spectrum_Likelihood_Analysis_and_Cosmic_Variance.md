# CMB Power Spectrum, Likelihood Analysis, and Cosmic Variance

Graduate lecture notes in Astro-Statistics and Cosmology, taught by Prof. Michele Liguori at the University of Padua.
Reference index [Astro-Statistics_and_Cosmology_MOC](../../../04_Atlas/Astro-Statistics_and_Cosmology_MOC.html)

---

## The Physics of Cosmic Microwave Background Anisotropies

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation provides our most pristine window into the early universe. Emitted at the epoch of recombination roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang ($z \approx 1100$), CMB photons traveled across the expanding cosmos largely unimpeded.

Observed across the full sky, the CMB exhibits an almost perfect blackbody spectrum with an average monopole temperature of $T_0 = 2.7255 \pm 0.0006 \, \text{K}$. Superimposed upon this uniform background are tiny fractional temperature anisotropies

$$\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}(\hat{n}) \equiv \frac{T(\hat{n}) - T_0}{T_0} \sim 10^{-5}$$

where $\hat{n} = (\theta, \phi)$ denotes the unit direction vector on the celestial sphere $\mathbb{S}^2$.

Under standard cosmological models supported by cosmic inflation, these temperature fluctuations arise from primordial Gaussian scalar perturbations generated by quantum fluctuations during the inflationary epoch. The temperature fluctuation field $\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}(\hat{n})$ is an isotropic, zero-mean Gaussian random field on the two-dimensional sphere.

---

## Spherical Harmonic Decomposition

Because the sky is a sphere, the natural orthonormal basis for decomposing scalar fields is the set of spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n})$, satisfying the orthogonality relation

$$\int_{\mathbb{S}^2} Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n}) \, Y_{\ell' m'}^*(\hat{n}) \, d\Omega = \delta_{\ell \ell'} \delta_{m m'}$$

where $d\Omega = \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\phi$.

Expand the temperature fluctuation field in spherical harmonic coefficients $a_{\ell m}$

$$\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}(\hat{n}) = \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell a_{\ell m} Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n})$$

The complex multipole coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ are obtained through the projection integral

$$a_{\ell m} = \int_{\mathbb{S}^2} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0}(\hat{n}) \, Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat{n}) \, d\Omega$$

Because the physical temperature field is strictly real, the multipole coefficients satisfy the reality condition

$$a_{\ell, -m} = (-1)^m a_{\ell m}^*$$

The multipole index $\ell$ corresponds to fluctuations on characteristic angular scales $\theta \approx \frac{180^\circ}{\ell} \approx \frac{\pi}{\ell}$.
- $\ell = 0$ is the monopole (overall average temperature).
- $\ell = 1$ is the dipole ($\theta \approx 180^\circ$), dominated by the kinematic Doppler shift of the Solar System moving relative to the CMB rest frame at $\approx 369 \, \text{km/s}$.
- $\ell = 2$ is the quadrupole ($\theta \approx 90^\circ$).
- $\ell \approx 220$ corresponds to the first acoustic peak ($\theta \approx 0.8^\circ$), mapping the sound horizon at last scattering.
- $\ell \gtrsim 1000$ probes sub-degree scales where Silk damping and gravitational lensing govern the spectrum.

---

## Statistical Isotropy and the Angular Power Spectrum $C_\ell$

The Cosmological Principle posits that on sufficiently large scales, the background universe is spatially homogeneous and isotropic.

Statistical isotropy implies that the statistical properties of the random field $\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}(\hat{n})$ are invariant under arbitrary three-dimensional spatial rotations of the celestial sphere.

Because the mean fluctuation vanishes by definition, the ensemble expectation value across all potential realizations of the universe is zero

$$\langle a_{\ell m} \rangle = 0$$

Rotational invariance places a rigid mathematical constraint on the two-point correlation matrix of the multipole coefficients. The covariance must be diagonal in both $\ell$ and $m$, and cannot depend on the azimuthal index $m$

$$\langle a_{\ell m} a_{\ell' m'}^* \rangle = C_\ell \, \delta_{\ell \ell'} \delta_{m m'}$$

The scalar sequence $\{C_\ell\}_{\ell=2}^\infty$ is the CMB angular power spectrum.

Because the primordial inflationary fluctuations are Gaussian, and linear gravitational perturbation theory governs their evolution prior to recombination, all higher-order connected correlation functions vanish. The angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ contains the complete statistical information of the cosmic microwave background.

---

## Estimating $C_\ell$ and the Cosmic Variance Limit

In observational cosmology, we do not have access to an ensemble of universes. We inhabit a single realization of the cosmos. We must estimate the theoretical ensemble variance $C_\ell$ from the specific $a_{\ell m}$ values realized in our observable patch of spacetime.

### The Unbiased Power Spectrum Estimator

For a fixed multipole $\ell$, the celestial sphere provides $2\ell + 1$ distinct $m$-modes ($m = -\ell, -\ell+1, \dots, \ell$). The natural estimator $\hat{C}_\ell$ is the empirical sample variance averaged across all available azimuthal modes

$$\hat{C}_\ell \equiv \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell |a_{\ell m}|^2$$

Taking the ensemble expectation value confirms that $\hat{C}_\ell$ is strictly unbiased

$$\langle \hat{C}_\ell \rangle = \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell \langle |a_{\ell m}|^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell C_\ell = C_\ell$$

### Cosmic Variance Derivation

Because the $a_{\ell m}$ are independent zero-mean Gaussian random variables with variance $C_\ell$, the quantity

$$X \equiv \frac{(2\ell + 1) \hat{C}_\ell}{C_\ell} = \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell \frac{|a_{\ell m}|^2}{C_\ell}$$

is a sum of squares of independent Gaussian variables, following a chi-squared distribution with $2\ell + 1$ degrees of freedom

$$X \sim \chi^2(2\ell + 1)$$

The variance of a chi-squared random variable with $\nu$ degrees of freedom is $2\nu$. Therefore

$$\operatorname{Var}\left( \frac{(2\ell + 1) \hat{C}_\ell}{C_\ell} \right) = 2(2\ell + 1)$$

Using the scaling property of variances $\operatorname{Var}(c Y) = c^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y)$

$$\frac{(2\ell + 1)^2}{C_\ell^2} \operatorname{Var}(\hat{C}_\ell) = 2(2\ell + 1)$$

Solving for the estimator variance yields

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{C}_\ell) = \frac{2}{2\ell + 1} C_\ell^2$$

The fractional standard error on the measured power spectrum is

$$\frac{\sigma(C_\ell)}{C_\ell} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{2\ell + 1}}$$

This statistical uncertainty is known as Cosmic Variance.

Cosmic variance represents an inescapable physical lower bound on measurement precision. It is not caused by detector noise, telescope optics, atmospheric absorption, or finite integration time. It arises solely because our universe provides only $2\ell + 1$ independent sample realizations for multipole $\ell$.
- For the quadrupole ($\ell = 2$), $2\ell + 1 = 5$ modes exist. The fractional cosmic variance uncertainty is $\sqrt{2/5} \approx 63.2\%$.
- For the octopole ($\ell = 3$), $2\ell + 1 = 7$ modes exist, giving $\sqrt{2/7} \approx 53.5\%$.
- At high multipoles ($\ell \approx 2000$), $2\ell + 1 \approx 4001$ modes exist, reducing cosmic variance to $\sqrt{2/4001} \approx 2.2\%$.

At low $\ell$, cosmic variance severely limits our ability to distinguish between alternative cosmological models that differ only at the largest scales (such as dark energy modifications or large-scale curvature).

## Sky Masking and the Knox Formula

In realistic observations, the full sky is not accessible. Intense diffuse Galactic emissions (interstellar dust thermal radiation, synchrotron emission from cosmic ray electrons, free-free emission from ionized gas) contaminate the Galactic plane. In addition, extragalactic bright radio sources and infrared dusty galaxies must be excised.

Let $W(\hat{n})$ denote the spatial masking window function ($W = 0$ in masked regions, $W = 1$ in clean cosmological sky regions). The effective fraction of usable sky is

$$f_{\text{sky}} \equiv \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{\mathbb{S}^2} W^2(\hat{n}) \, d\Omega$$

Masking breaks the rotational symmetry of the sky. As a consequence, spherical harmonics are no longer orthogonal over the cut sky, inducing mode-coupling between different multipoles $\ell$ and $\ell'$ (pseudo-$C_\ell$ formalism).

Furthermore, any real instrument introduces experimental noise and finite optical resolution.
- Instrumental Beam - an optical aperture convolves the sky with a point spread function, typically modeled as a Gaussian beam of full-width at half-maximum $\theta_{\text{FWHM}}$. In harmonic space, this multiplies the signal by the beam window function $B_\ell = \exp\left( -\frac{\ell(\ell + 1) \theta_{\text{beam}}^2}{2} \right)$, where $\theta_{\text{beam}} = \frac{\theta_{\text{FWHM}}}{\sqrt{8\ln 2}}$.
- Instrumental Noise - detector noise adds an uncorrelated power spectrum component $N_\ell = \sigma_{\text{pix}}^2 \Omega_{\text{pix}}$.

Knox (1995) combined these physical effects to derive the total variance on the reconstructed power spectrum

$$\sigma(C_\ell) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{(2\ell + 1) f_{\text{sky}}}} \left( C_\ell + \frac{N_\ell}{B_\ell^2} \right)$$

This expression reveals two distinct observational regimes
1. Signal-Dominated (Cosmic Variance Limited) Regime - when $C_\ell \gg N_\ell / B_\ell^2$, detector noise is negligible. The error is governed entirely by sample variance, degraded by the cut-sky geometric factor $1/\sqrt{f_{\text{sky}}}$.
2. Noise-Dominated Regime - at small angular scales (high $\ell$), the beam function $B_\ell$ suppresses the cosmological signal exponentially, while detector noise remains flat. The noise term explodes, terminating the useful cosmological reach of the telescope.

## The Exact CMB Likelihood Function

To infer cosmological parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ from measured CMB maps, we must construct the likelihood function $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \equiv p(\boldsymbol{d} | \boldsymbol{\theta})$.

### Full-Sky Ideal Likelihood

Assume an idealized full-sky observation without noise. Given theoretical spectrum $C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, the multipole coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ are independent zero-mean complex Gaussian variables.

The joint probability density of all modes up to $\ell_{\text{max}}$ is

$$p(\{a_{\ell m}\} | \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\text{max}}} \prod_{m=-\ell}^\ell \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})}} \exp\left( -\frac{|a_{\ell m}|^2}{2 C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})} \right)$$

Substituting the estimator $\hat{C}_\ell = \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} \sum_{m} |a_{\ell m}|^2$, the likelihood function factorizes into

$$\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\text{max}}} \frac{1}{\left[ 2\pi C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right]^{(2\ell + 1)/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{2\ell + 1}{2} \frac{\hat{C}_\ell}{C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})} \right)$$

Taking the natural logarithm yields the exact log-likelihood

$$-2 \ln \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{\ell=2}^{\ell_{\text{max}}} (2\ell + 1) \left[ \ln\left( \frac{C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\hat{C}_\ell} \right) + \frac{\hat{C}_\ell}{C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})} - 1 \right] + \text{const}$$

This is the exact inverted Wishart / gamma log-likelihood.

### Asymptotic Gaussianity at High $\ell$

Notice that for small $\ell$ (such as $\ell = 2, 3$), the log-likelihood is highly asymmetric and non-Gaussian, displaying an extended tail toward large $C_\ell$. This asymmetry reflects the underlying chi-squared distribution with few degrees of freedom.

However, as $\ell$ increases, $2\ell + 1 \gg 1$. Let $\delta C_\ell \equiv \hat{C}_\ell - C_\ell$. Expanding the logarithm to second order in $\frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell}$

$$\ln\left( \frac{C_\ell}{\hat{C}_\ell} \right) = -\ln\left( 1 + \frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} \right) \approx -\frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} \right)^2$$

Substituting this into the bracketed term

$$\ln\left( \frac{C_\ell}{\hat{C}_\ell} \right) + \frac{\hat{C}_\ell}{C_\ell} - 1 \approx -\frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} \right)^2 + \left( 1 + \frac{\delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} \right) - 1 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\hat{C}_\ell - C_\ell}{C_\ell} \right)^2$$

The log-likelihood reduces smoothly to a Gaussian form

$$-2 \ln \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \approx \sum_{\ell} \frac{[\hat{C}_\ell - C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})]^2}{\frac{2}{2\ell + 1} C_\ell^2} = \sum_{\ell} \frac{[\hat{C}_\ell - C_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})]^2}{\sigma^2(C_\ell)}$$

### Real Survey Likelihood Architecture

In modern cosmological analysis pipelines (such as Planck 2018 or ACT/SPT), computing the likelihood cannot rely on a single simple formula across the entire multipole range. Instead, pipelines implement a hybrid architecture
1. Low-$\ell$ Likelihood ($\ell < 30$) - because the sky cut and non-Gaussian cosmic variance are severe, the likelihood operates in pixel space or through exact Gibbs sampling / Blackwell-Rao estimators, solving the joint temperature and polarization covariance matrix directly.
2. High-$\ell$ Likelihood ($\ell \ge 30$) - because $2\ell + 1 \gg 1$, the Central Limit Theorem holds. The likelihood is modeled as a multivariate Gaussian over binned pseudo-$C_\ell$ bandpowers, with a precomputed covariance matrix accounting for mode-coupling, beam errors, and foreground templates.
