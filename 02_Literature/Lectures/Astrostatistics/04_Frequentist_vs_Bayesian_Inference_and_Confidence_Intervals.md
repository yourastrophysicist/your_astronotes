# Frequentist versus Bayesian Inference and Confidence Interval Subtleties

## The Foundational Epistemological Divide

The debate between frequentist and Bayesian approaches to statistical inference is not a mere technical dispute over computational recipes. It reflects two fundamentally distinct epistemological stances on the meaning of probability and the nature of scientific evidence.

In the frequentist formulation, probability represents the objective limiting relative frequency of an event in an infinite sequence of hypothetical, independent, identically repeated trials. A true parameter of nature (such as the matter density $\Omega_m$ or the amplitude of primordial scalar fluctuations $A_s$) is a fixed, deterministic constant. It possesses no probability distribution. Only random variables generated by repeating the experiment possess distributions.

In the Bayesian formulation, probability is a formal quantitative measure of degree of belief or plausibility given incomplete information. The actual observed data vector $\boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}}$ is the only concrete reality in hand. The physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are uncertain, and this state of uncertainty is quantified by probability density functions conditioned on the observations.

## Frequentist Point Estimation and Classical Desiderata

In the frequentist paradigm, parameter estimation begins by proposing an estimator. An estimator $\hat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{d})$ is a deterministic function of the random data vector $\boldsymbol{d}$. Because the data fluctuate across hypothetical repeated experiments, the estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is itself a random variable with a sampling distribution $p(\hat{\theta} \mid \theta_{\text{true}})$.

Classical frequentist statistics evaluates estimators based on average properties over hypothetical repetitions.

### Bias

The bias of an estimator is the difference between its expected value across infinite repetitions and the true underlying parameter value

$$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) \equiv \langle \hat{\theta} \rangle - \theta_{\text{true}} = \int \hat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{d}) \, p(\boldsymbol{d} \mid \theta_{\text{true}}) \, d\boldsymbol{d} - \theta_{\text{true}}$$

An estimator is unbiased if $\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = 0$ for all possible values of $\theta_{\text{true}}$.

### Variance and Mean Squared Error

The sampling variance measures the dispersion of the estimator about its expected value

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \equiv \left\langle (\hat{\theta} - \langle \hat{\theta} \rangle)^2 \right\rangle$$

The total quality of an estimator is quantified by its Mean Squared Error (MSE)

$$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) \equiv \left\langle (\hat{\theta} - \theta_{\text{true}})^2 \right\rangle = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \left[ \operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) \right]^2$$

This decomposition highlights the bias-variance tradeoff. Imposing strict unbiasedness is not always optimal. Often, introducing a minor controlled bias (as in Ridge regression or Bayesian shrinkage priors) reduces the variance substantially, yielding a smaller total mean squared error.

### Consistency and Efficiency

An estimator is consistent if it converges in probability to the true parameter value as the sample size $N$ approaches infinity

$$\lim_{N \to \infty} P( \mid \hat{\theta}_N - \theta_{\text{true}} \mid > \epsilon) = 0 \quad \text{for any } \epsilon > 0$$

An unbiased estimator is efficient if its variance achieves the theoretical lower bound established by the Cramér-Rao inequality.

## Neyman Construction of Frequentist Confidence Intervals

One of the most widely misunderstood concepts in physical science is the distinction between a frequentist confidence interval and a Bayesian credible interval.

Jerzy Neyman (1937) formulated the classical confidence interval to provide statements regarding parameters without assigning probability distributions to them.

### The Formal Coverage Property

Let $\boldsymbol{d}$ be an observed dataset drawn from $p(\boldsymbol{d} \mid \theta_{\text{true}})$. A frequentist confidence interval at confidence level $1 - \alpha$ consists of two data-dependent boundary functions $L(\boldsymbol{d})$ and $U(\boldsymbol{d})$ constructed such that across hypothetical infinite repetitions of the experiment, the random interval $[L(\boldsymbol{d}), U(\boldsymbol{d})]$ covers the fixed true value $\theta_{\text{true}}$ with probability at least $1 - \alpha$

$$P\left( L(\boldsymbol{d}) \le \theta_{\text{true}} \le U(\boldsymbol{d}) \, \middle| \, \theta_{\text{true}} \right) = 1 - \alpha \quad \text{for all possible } \theta_{\text{true}}$$

Notice the critical mathematical detail. In this equation, $\theta_{\text{true}}$ is a fixed, non-random number. The random variables are the lower and upper bounds $L(\boldsymbol{d})$ and $U(\boldsymbol{d})$.

Once an astronomer carries out a specific real experiment and collects a concrete dataset $\boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}}$, evaluating the functions produces two fixed numerical numbers $[L(\boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}}), U(\boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}})]$ (for example, $[67.4, 72.1]$ for $H_0$).

In strict frequentist logic, one cannot assert that "there is a 95% probability that the true $H_0$ lies between 67.4 and 72.1". The true constant $H_0$ is either inside that specific numerical interval, or it is outside. The probability is either 1 or 0, but remains unknown. The 95% figure refers exclusively to the long-run success rate of the statistical procedure over an imaginary infinite ensemble of future identical experiments.

### The Bayesian Credible Interval

In direct contrast, a Bayesian credible interval conditions strictly on the observed data $\boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}}$

$$P\left( \theta \in [L, U] \, \middle \mid \, \boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}} \right) = \int_L^U p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{d}_{\text{obs}}) \, d\theta = 1 - \alpha$$

Here, the boundaries $L$ and $U$ are fixed numbers computed from the posterior distribution. The parameter $\theta$ is the random variable described by our state of knowledge. The Bayesian statement matches what scientists intuitively want to conclude. Given the concrete data collected by our instrument, there is a $100(1 - \alpha)\%$ probability that the true physical parameter lies between $L$ and $U$.

## Subtle Paradoxes and Divergences

The fundamental philosophical division produces stark operational divergences in specific observational scenarios.

### The Stopping Rule Paradox and the Likelihood Principle

Consider an experiment designed to measure the success rate $p$ of a rare cosmic event (such as detecting an optical flash associated with a fast radio burst). Suppose the experiment records $N = 12$ attempts, yielding $k = 3$ successful detections and 9 null events.

Now consider two distinct experimental protocols that could have produced this exact identical dataset.

Protocol 1 (Fixed Sample Size). The experimenter obtained a telescope allocation granted in advance for exactly $N = 12$ observations. Here $N$ is fixed, and the number of successes $k$ is the random variable, following a Binomial distribution

$$p(k \mid p, N=12) = \binom{12}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{12 - k} = \binom{12}{3} p^3 (1 - p)^9 = 220 \, p^3 (1 - p)^9$$

Protocol 2 (Fixed Number of Successes). The experimenter decided to observe continuously until exactly $k = 3$ successes were recorded, at which point funding ran out. Here $k = 3$ is fixed, and the total number of observations $N$ is the random variable, following a Negative Binomial distribution

$$p(N \mid p, k=3) = \binom{N - 1}{k - 1} p^k (1 - p)^{N - k} = \binom{11}{2} p^3 (1 - p)^9 = 55 \, p^3 (1 - p)^9$$

Now evaluate the scientific inferences under both frameworks.

In Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution depends only on the likelihood function up to an overall normalization constant. Notice that as a function of the parameter $p$, both likelihoods are strictly proportional to $p^3 (1 - p)^9$. The constant combinatorial prefactors (220 versus 55) cancel out completely in Bayes' theorem. Assuming identical priors, the Bayesian posterior $p(p \mid \boldsymbol{d})$ is identical under both protocols. The internal intentions or stopping rules of the experimenter do not alter the physical inference drawn from the recorded data.

In frequentist hypothesis testing, however, significance testing requires computing the $p$-value, which sums the probabilities of unobserved data points at least as extreme as the observed data.
- Under Protocol 1 (Binomial), the probability of observing 3 or fewer successes under the null hypothesis $H_0: p = 0.5$ is $\sum_{j=0}^3 \binom{12}{j} (0.5)^{12} \approx 0.073$.
- Under Protocol 2 (Negative Binomial), the probability of requiring 12 or more trials to achieve 3 successes under $H_0: p = 0.5$ is $\sum_{m=12}^\infty \binom{m-1}{2} (0.5)^m \approx 0.032$.

Under Protocol 1, the frequentist accepts the null hypothesis at the 5% level ($p = 0.073 > 0.05$). Under Protocol 2, the frequentist rejects the null hypothesis at the 5% level ($p = 0.032 < 0.05$).

Two experimenters sitting in the same observatory, looking at the exact same log of 3 detections in 12 trials, reach opposing scientific conclusions simply because their intended stopping criteria differed.

This illustrates the Likelihood Principle, which states that all information about an unknown parameter contained in an observational dataset is conveyed entirely by the likelihood function. Bayesian inference adheres strictly to the Likelihood Principle; frequentist $p$-values violate it because they integrate over hypothetical data that were never observed.

### Physical Boundaries and Empty Confidence Intervals

Another critical failure mode of classical frequentist confidence intervals occurs in bounded parameter spaces.

Suppose an experiment measures the square of the neutrino mass $\theta = m_\nu^2$, where physical consistency strictly demands $\theta \ge 0$. Suppose the measurement yields a Gaussian distribution $d \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma = 1.0 \, \text{eV}^2$.

Suppose the experimental instrument records a large downward statistical fluctuation, yielding $d = -2.5 \, \text{eV}^2$.

Constructing a standard 95% frequentist confidence interval $[d - 1.96\sigma, d + 1.96\sigma]$ gives the interval $[-4.46, -0.54] \, \text{eV}^2$.

This standard frequentist interval lies entirely within the unphysical realm. If the experimenter truncates the interval to the physical domain $[0, \infty)$, the resulting interval is empty. Reporting that "the true physical neutrino mass is enclosed within the empty set with 95% confidence" is scientifically absurd.

In the Bayesian framework, physical boundaries are incorporated from the outset through the prior density, setting $\pi(\theta) = 0$ for $\theta < 0$. The posterior probability density automatically vanishes in the unphysical sector, and the resulting credible interval will naturally lie entirely within the physically permissible region $[0, \theta_{\text{upper}}]$, properly conveying that the downward noise fluctuation provides a tight upper limit on the mass.

### Lindley's Paradox

Lindley's paradox demonstrates a striking discrepancy between frequentist hypothesis tests and Bayesian model comparisons in large sample sizes.

Consider testing a precise null hypothesis $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ against a broad alternative hypothesis $H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0$, using a large dataset of $N$ independent measurements.

As the sample size $N$ becomes massive, the standard error of the mean $\sigma / \sqrt{N}$ becomes tiny. A sample mean $\bar{d}$ that differs from $\theta_0$ by a slight amount (say, $2.1$ standard errors) will produce a frequentist $p$-value around $0.036$, rejecting $H_0$ at the standard 5% significance level.

However, in the Bayesian calculation, the alternative hypothesis $H_1$ spreads its prior probability mass over a broad parameter domain $\Delta\theta$. Because $\Delta\theta \gg \sigma/\sqrt{N}$, the likelihood under $H_1$ averaged across the prior is suppressed by the Occam factor. Consequently, the Bayes factor can overwhelmingly favor the null hypothesis $H_0$ over $H_1$, even while the frequentist rejects $H_0$ at $p < 0.05$.

This paradox underscores that statistical significance in the frequentist sense does not measure evidence for a physical discovery when sample sizes are large.
