title: "Galactic GC two-population age structure"
---
when you compile homogeneous ages for galactic globular clusters and look at their age distribution rather than the age-metallicity relation, you see **bimodality**. there is an old peak around $12.5$-$13$ Gyr and a younger peak around $10$-$12$ Gyr. this is not an artifact of binning or isochrone choice (it shows up in marin-franch et al. 2009, dotter et al. 2010/2011, vandenberg et al. 2013, and leaman et al. 2013) and it has a clean physical interpretation.

**old branch (in situ)**: clusters at $\sim 12.5$-$13$ Gyr. these are predominantly metal-poor halo clusters at small galactocentric radius, plus the metal-rich bulge clusters (47 Tuc-like). they trace the rapid, coherent star formation episode in the early milky way potential, the same episode that built the bulge and the inner halo (see [Galactic halo and bulge formation timescales](Galactic%20halo%20and%20bulge%20formation%20timescales.html)). the small age dispersion within this branch (a few hundred Myr) means the formation was fast.

**young branch (accreted)**: clusters at $\sim 10$-$12$ Gyr. these tend to live at larger $R_{GC}$, often have horizontal-branch morphologies that are too red for their metallicities (the "second-parameter" clusters), and many are kinematically linked to known tidal substructure: the **sagittarius dwarf stream** brought in M54, ter 7, ter 8, arp 2, pal 12, and probably whiting 1; **gaia-enceladus** is associated with another dozen clusters identified by massari, koppelman & helmi (2019) using gaia DR2 actions; the **sequoia** progenitor with another handful; the **helmi streams** with a few more. dwarf-galaxy progenitors had slower star formation, so their oldest clusters are still old but their typical clusters are 1-2 Gyr younger than the in-situ ones at the same $[\text{Fe/H}]$.

a useful way to visualise this: in the age-$[\text{Fe/H}]$ plane (see [Age-metallicity relation of Galactic GCs](Age-metallicity%20relation%20of%20Galactic%20GCs.html)), the in-situ branch is nearly vertical at old age and slopes only slightly down with metallicity, while the accreted branch is shallower and offset to younger ages. the two branches **cross or overlap in metallicity around $[\text{Fe/H}] \sim -1.5$**, so age alone or metallicity alone is insufficient to classify a cluster. the joint distribution does the trick, especially with kinematics from gaia.

quantitative numbers worth keeping in mind:

- in situ branch median: $t \approx 12.8$ Gyr.
- accreted branch median: $t \approx 11$ Gyr, with $\sigma \sim 0.8$ Gyr.
- fraction of accreted GCs in the milky way system: $\sim 30$-$50\%$ depending on classifier (kinematics versus chemistry versus age).
- gaia-enceladus alone contributes $\sim 20\%$ of the halo GC system.

**second parameter problem**, briefly: at fixed $[\text{Fe/H}]$, the morphology of the horizontal branch varies. age is the leading second parameter (older clusters have bluer HBs at fixed metallicity), but other parameters like helium abundance, mass loss on the RGB, and central density also play a role. the accreted clusters being systematically younger explains why outer-halo clusters have redder HBs than inner-halo clusters at the same $[\text{Fe/H}]$.

the two-population age structure is one of the strongest direct pieces of evidence for the hierarchical assembly of the milky way halo. it is consistent with $\Lambda$CDM predictions for halo build-up via mergers and provides a stellar-cluster-based test that complements field-star kinematics from gaia.
