# CMB angular power spectrum estimator and cosmic variance

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) temperature anisotropy field $\Delta T(\hat{n})$ on the celestial sphere is our primary cosmological window into the early universe. Its statistical properties are encoded in the angular power spectrum $C_\ell$, whose precision is fundamentally limited at large angular scales by cosmic variance.

## Spherical Harmonic Decomposition

Let the fractional temperature anisotropy in direction $\hat{n} = (\theta, \phi)$ be expanded in spherical harmonics:
$$\frac{\Delta T(\hat{n})}{T_0} = \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell a_{\ell m} Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n})$$
where $T_0 = 2.7255\text{ K}$, and $a_{\ell m}$ are the complex multipole coefficients:
$$a_{\ell m} = \int_{S^2} \frac{\Delta T(\hat{n})}{T_0} Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat{n}) \, d\Omega$$

Assuming statistical isotropy and homogeneity generated by linear Gaussian primordial perturbations:
$$\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell m}] = 0$$
$$\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell m} a_{\ell' m'}^*] = C_\ell \delta_{\ell \ell'} \delta_{m m'}$$
where $C_\ell$ is the true theoretical angular power spectrum.

## The Unbiased Estimator

For an individual realization of the universe, each multipole $\ell$ provides $2\ell + 1$ independent $m$-modes ($m \in [-\ell, \ell]$). The standard estimator $\hat{C}_\ell$ is:
$$\hat{C}_\ell \equiv \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell \lvert a_{\ell m}\rvert^2$$

Taking the expectation value:
$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}_\ell] = \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} \sum_{m=-\ell}^\ell \mathbb{E}[\lvert a_{\ell m}\rvert^2] = \frac{1}{2\ell + 1} (2\ell + 1) C_\ell = C_\ell$$
confirming that $\hat{C}_\ell$ is an unbiased estimator.

## Derivation of Cosmic Variance

To calculate the variance $\text{Var}(\hat{C}_\ell) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{C}_\ell^2] - (\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}_\ell])^2$:
$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}_\ell^2] = \frac{1}{(2\ell+1)^2} \sum_{m, m'} \mathbb{E}[\lvert a_{\ell m}\rvert^2 \lvert a_{\ell m'}\rvert^2]$$

By Wick's theorem for a zero-mean Gaussian random field:
$$\mathbb{E}[a_1 a_2^* a_3 a_4^*] = \mathbb{E}[a_1 a_2^*]\mathbb{E}[a_3 a_4^*] + \mathbb{E}[a_1 a_3]\mathbb{E}[a_2^* a_4^*] + \mathbb{E}[a_1 a_4^*]\mathbb{E}[a_2^* a_3]$$

Applying Wick's theorem:
$$\mathbb{E}[\lvert a_{\ell m}\rvert^2 \lvert a_{\ell m'}\rvert^2] = C_\ell^2 (1 + \delta_{m m'})$$
$$\sum_{m, m'} \mathbb{E}[\lvert a_{\ell m}\rvert^2 \lvert a_{\ell m'}\rvert^2] = (2\ell+1)^2 C_\ell^2 + (2\ell+1) C_\ell^2$$

Subtracting $(\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}_\ell])^2 = C_\ell^2$:
$$\text{Var}(\hat{C}_\ell) = \frac{2}{2\ell + 1} C_\ell^2$$

The fractional uncertainty defines the irreducible **cosmic variance limit**:
$$\frac{\Delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{2\ell + 1}}$$

At the quadrupole ($\ell = 2$), $2\ell + 1 = 5$, giving $\Delta C_2 / C_2 = \sqrt{2/5} \approx 63.2\%$. No future telescope or detector can ever reduce this error, because our universe contains only five independent $m$-modes on the sky.

## Sky Masking and the Knox Formula

Galactic dust and point source contamination require masking the Milky Way plane, observing only a fractional sky coverage $f_{\rm sky} < 1$. Knox (1995) showed that including instrumental noise $N_\ell$:
$$\Delta C_\ell = \sqrt{\frac{2}{(2\ell + 1) f_{\rm sky}}} \left( C_\ell + N_\ell \right)$$
where $N_\ell = w^{-1} e^{\ell(\ell+1)\theta_{\rm beam}^2 / (8\ln 2)}$ accounts for beam smearing and detector noise weight $w$.
