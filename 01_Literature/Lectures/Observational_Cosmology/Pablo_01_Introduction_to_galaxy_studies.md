# introduction to galaxy studies — Pablo Pérez-González, lecture 0

*visiting professor, Observational Cosmology A.Y. 2024/2025, block: 31 March – 3 April*

goal of the lecture, as written on the slide: **historical introduction to the study of galaxies.** that is the whole thing. no derivations, no equations — just the long walk from a small cloud next to Andromeda's mouth, drawn by a Persian astronomer in the tenth century, to JWST's NIRCam staring down the first billion years.

index: [Observational_Cosmology_MOC](../../../00_Atlas/Observational_Cosmology_MOC.md)

---

## the claim pablo is making

extragalactic astrophysics is younger than it feels. it is about a hundred years old. the idea that there are other galaxies at all — not just faint cloud-like bits of the milky way, but entire separate universes of stars — was only *settled* in 1924, with hubble's cepheids in M33 and M31.

everything we now call cosmology rides on that realization. so the history of galaxy studies is also, sneakily, the history of cosmology.

---

## before the telescope: naked-eye galaxies

there are only a handful of galaxies visible without optics: the **LMC**, the **SMC**, and **Andromeda (M31)**. Andromeda is faint and diffuse, and for most of history nobody had any reason to think it was anything other than a cloud inside our sky.

the first real entry is **Al-Sūfī (903–986)** in his *Book of Fixed Stars*: he draws andromeda as a "small cloud" on the mouth of the constellation figure. that drawing is, as far as we know, the first documented observation of another galaxy. nobody calls it a galaxy yet. it is just a cloud that behaves strangely.

Democritus, much earlier, had proposed that the Milky Way itself was made of stars too faint to resolve individually. that turned out to be right, and it stays the dominant idea until the invention of the telescope.

---

## galileo and the resolved milky way

**Galileo (1564–1642)** points his telescope at the milky way and sees it dissolve into stars. that is the first confirmation that Democritus was right — the milky way is not a glowing river, it is a population. but galileo can only see three of the galaxies brighter than magnitude 8, and they are all diffuse and hard to study. the naked eye (and the early telescope) just does not have the throughput to do extragalactic work.

---

## kant, wright, and the island-universe conjecture

**Thomas Wright (1711–1786)** proposes that the milky way has a definite flat geometry. **Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)** extends it philosophically: maybe the milky way is one of many **island universes** — flat stellar systems like our own, rotating under the same physics that keeps planets in solar-system orbits. kant says the other island universes would be too far to resolve into stars, and would appear to us as diffuse nebulae.

this is the correct answer, a century and a half too early. kant has no evidence. but the vocabulary he invents — *Weltinsel*, island universe — is exactly the phrase the Great Debate will settle in 1920.

---

## the cataloguers: messier, the herschels, dreyer

**Charles Messier (1730–1817)** is hunting comets and keeps getting distracted by fuzzy things that are not comets. so he writes them down to *avoid* them: 25 objects, then 103, eventually 110 (the last 7 added in 1966 from his notes). the messier catalog is a mixed bag — galaxies, globular clusters, planetary nebulae, supernova remnants — but it is the first usable list of extended objects in the northern sky.

**William Herschel (1738–1822)** and his sister **Caroline (1750–1848)** push it further: 2,500 nebulae, fainter than messier's. william also draws the first map of the milky way, putting the sun at the center (wrong, but reasonable given what he could see).

the list gets extended by **John Herschel (1792–1871)** and finally published in 1888 by **J.L.E. Dreyer (1852–1926)** as the **New General Catalogue (NGC)**: 7,840 objects. in 1895 dreyer adds the **Index Catalog (IC)** with 5,387 more.

now we have tens of thousands of nebulae on the sky, clumped strangely — for example the **Virgo cluster**, a tight knot of M87, M86, M84, M89, and dozens of NGC sources. nobody knows yet that these clumpings *are* clusters of galaxies, but the data is already sitting in the catalogs.

---

## rosse and the spirals

**William Parsons, Earl of Rosse (1800–1867)**, builds the **Leviathan of Parsonstown** in 1845, the largest telescope of the 19th century. with it he resolves spiral structure. his drawing of M51 is the first time anyone has seen that a nebula has arms. he also draws M33 as a loose whorl (1846). the word *nebula* starts to fracture: some of them are clearly structured, clearly not just featureless clouds.

---

## huggins and the two kinds of nebulae

**William Huggins (1824–1910)** in 1864 takes the first spectra of nebulae and finds two populations:

- some show **emission lines** — hot gas (these are planetary nebulae, HII regions)
- some show **absorption lines like stars** — these are galaxies, made of many unresolved stars

**J. Scheiner's 1899** paper publishes the first high-quality spectrum of M31 in *ApJ* vol. 5. his own words: *"the thought suggests itself of comparing these systems with our stellar system, with especial reference to its great similarity to the Andromeda nebula."* he is already leaning toward kant's answer.

---

## the great debate: shapley vs curtis, 1920

by 1917, **Heber Curtis (1872–1942)** at Lick Observatory has found 11 novae in M31. they are ten magnitudes fainter than milky-way novae, which (for the same intrinsic brightness) puts M31 at about **150 kpc** — well outside the galaxy. curtis argues these are **island universes**. other evidence he uses: dust lanes in other nebulae match the dust lanes in the MW; redshifts of spiral nebulae imply velocities larger than the MW escape velocity.

**Harlow Shapley (1885–1972)** argues the opposite. he has just used cepheid variables (via **Henrietta Swan Leavitt's** 1908 period-luminosity law) to measure the milky way's size and to move the sun *off* its center. he argues the MW is gigantic (100 million ly across, he says), and that andromeda at that distance would be absurdly large. **Adriaan van Maanen (1884–1946)** backs him with (incorrect) measurements of the rotation of M101's disk — if M101 were truly distant, those rotation speeds would exceed $c$.

in 1920 the two debate at the Smithsonian. general opinion at the time: shapley wins. in retrospect: curtis was right on the main question (island universes exist) but wrong on MW size and sun position — shapley was right on those.

history rarely picks a clean winner.

---

## lundmark, duncan, hubble — 1921–1924

the debate is settled in the next five years, quietly, by better data.

- **Knut Lundmark (1889–1958)** in 1921: the density of 15.7-mag stars in the M33 field is ordinary. so M33 is farther than a typical star of that magnitude (3 kpc). if M33's brightest star is as bright as the brightest star in the MW, M33 must be at ≈ **300 kpc** — three times the size shapley gave for the MW itself.
- **J.C. Duncan** in 1922: first observation of variable stars in M33.
- **Edwin Hubble (1889–1953)** in 1924: 47 variables in M33, 22 of them cepheids. their periods give distances of 285 kpc (modern values: ≈ 900 kpc for M33, ≈ 800 kpc for M31). **M33 and M31 are galaxies.**

this is the moment extragalactic astrophysics begins.

---

## hubble 1929 and hubble 1936

- **1929**: hubble publishes the distance–velocity relation — cepheid distances plotted against redshifts measured from spectra. this is **hubble's law**, $v = H_0 d$. the universe is expanding. (note: modern pedagogy more honestly calls it the Hubble–Lemaître law — see below.)
- **1936**: hubble publishes his morphological classification of nearby galaxies. the tuning fork (ellipticals, S0, spirals Sa/Sb/Sc, barred SBa/SBb/SBc, irregulars) is still in use.

---

## lemaître and einstein — the theoretical half

in parallel:

- **Albert Einstein (1879–1955)** in 1917 applies GR to the universe. his field equations predict an expanding or contracting universe, which he considered philosophically wrong, so he inserts the **cosmological constant Λ** to make it static.
- **Georges Lemaître (1894–1966)** in 1927 publishes (in a small Belgian journal, in french) *"a homogeneous Universe of constant mass and increasing radius that explains the recession velocity of extragalactic nebulae."* this is essentially hubble's law two years before hubble. einstein says: *"your mathematics is correct but your physics is abominable."*
- lemaître's own 1927 paper has $H_0 \approx 625$ km/s/Mpc — enormous compared to the modern ≈ 70, because the early cepheid calibration was off by about a factor of ten. but the *form* of the relation is right.
- **Arthur Eddington (1882–1944)** finally gives lemaître his due in a 1930 MNRAS review — and translates the 1927 paper into english.

the rest of the 20th century's cosmology vocabulary then lands: **Friedmann (1888–1925)**, **Robertson (1903–1961)**, **Walker (1909–2001)**, **Gamow (1904–1968)**, **Hoyle (1915–2001)** — hoyle invents the term "big bang" as a *criticism* of non-stationary models, and the name sticks despite him.

---

## 1930s onward: the cosmological boom

after 1930 extragalactic astrophysics explodes: deeper galaxies, more spectra, more theoretical models. pablo singles out:

- **Chandrasekhar (1943)** — stochastic problems
- **de Vaucouleurs (1948)** — morphological classification beyond hubble
- **Dirac (1937)** — cosmological constants, large-numbers hypothesis
- **Seyfert (1943)** — the AGN class that now bears his name
- **Zwicky (1937)** — galaxy masses, the first dark-matter argument

then:

- **1965** — **Penzias & Wilson** discover the **cosmic microwave background**, the decisive evidence for hot big bang. this is block 6 of the course.
- **1974** — **Vera Rubin (1928–…)** measures flat rotation curves of spiral galaxies and postulates dark matter as a dynamically required, optically invisible component.
- **1980s–1990s** — **Alan Guth** and **Andrei Linde** develop **inflation** to solve the flatness, horizon, and monopole problems (block 7).
- **2011 Nobel** — **Perlmutter** and **Schmidt** (and Riess) get the prize for evidence of **dark energy** from type Ia supernovae. this will come back hard in Rodighiero's chapter 0 when we derive the $\Lambda$-dominated Friedmann solution.

---

## the last 30 years — why we can now do this at all

sensitivity. that is it. that is the whole story of modern extragalactic astrophysics.

the instruments pablo wants us to remember:

- **space**: HST, Chandra, XMM-Newton, Spitzer, Herschel, JWST
- **ground, ≥ 10 m**: Keck, VLT, Subaru, LBT, GTC, ALMA
- **upcoming**: ELT (39 m), SKA

the plots at the end of the historical slides are already dated "circa 2010" — they show survey volume vs redshift (2dFGRS, SDSS-DR6, WiggleZ, DEEP-2, BOSS LRGs, the then-future WFMOS at $z=1$) and source density vs area. even that 2010 snapshot already dwarfs every galaxy count before 1990 by orders of magnitude. since then JWST has flipped the picture at $z \gtrsim 10$ again.

## what i take from this lecture

- extragalactic astrophysics is a chain of technological limits breaking one after another. naked eye → telescope (galileo) → bigger telescope (rosse's leviathan) → photography (messier, the herschels) → spectroscopy (huggins) → large reflectors and cepheids (hubble) → radio + CMB (penzias–wilson) → space observatories + 10-m class → JWST. every big conceptual jump — island universes, expansion, dark matter, dark energy, reionization — is downstream of a new instrument.
- the history here is not decoration. Rodighiero's chapter 0 is going to derive $(1+z) = a(t_e)^{-1}$, $d_L = d_A(1+z)^2$, $q_0 = \Omega_m/2 - \Omega_\Lambda$ — and every one of those equations is the formalization of something somebody first *observed* in the timeline above. the redshift is huggins and slipher. the luminosity distance is hubble's 1929 plot. the $\Lambda$ term is perlmutter/schmidt.
- the "great debate" is a lesson in why distance indicators matter more than anything else. curtis and shapley had the same sky and disagreed about the size of the universe by three orders of magnitude. the dispute was settled by *one* working standard candle (cepheids).

next lecture will be where pablo gets technical — morphology, luminosity function, SEDs, photo-z — and that is where my [Observational_Cosmology_MOC](../../../00_Atlas/Observational_Cosmology_MOC.md) will start to grow under block 10.

## terms to remember going forward

- **island universe** — historical phrase for "external galaxy" (kant, wright, curtis)
- **NGC / IC** — the big 19th-century catalogs (dreyer)
- **great debate** — shapley vs curtis, 1920, Smithsonian
- **cepheid variable** — period–luminosity (leavitt) → distance ladder (hubble)
- **hubble–lemaître law** — $v = H_0 d$, 1927 (lemaître) / 1929 (hubble)
- **cosmological constant** — einstein 1917, later revived for dark energy
- **CMB** — penzias & wilson 1965
- **dark matter** — zwicky 1937, rubin 1974
- **dark energy** — perlmutter/schmidt/riess, supernova cosmology, nobel 2011
